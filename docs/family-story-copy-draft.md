## `/stories` introduction

I began looking for the biological family I was born into. The records have carried that search from Benjamin and Hannah Meason in Kentucky, through Missouri and Texas, and eventually to me. They have also led me into a much wider family: people named together in wills, claiming the same land, sharing households, working nearby, and carrying names across generations.

Some connections are stated plainly in the records. Others become visible only when several records are read together. And some—most notably the identity of Benjamin's parents—remain open. These chapters follow the family the evidence lets me see, including the places where it stops. This history adds to the story of the Cohen family that raised me; it does not replace it.

## New Joseph chapter: _A deed out of reach_

### Opening

On 29 March 1799, Joseph Meason was very sick aboard a Kentucky boat on the Monongahela River, about a mile above Pittsburgh. Mary Hartle later testified that he appeared to be in his right mind. About an hour before he died, Joseph asked for a deed he had drawn to convey land to Joseph Worthington. The document could not be reached among the packed belongings aboard the boat.

Someone asked whether Joseph could state his wishes before witnesses instead. He did. Hartle and two other witnesses later gave depositions, and a Kentucky court ordered those accounts recorded. In their testimony, Joseph's wishes concerned his estate and, specifically, a thousand acres on Highland Creek.

The court record tells us Joseph was sick when Hartle came aboard and died less than an hour after speaking. It does not tell us what made him ill, where the boat had come from, where it was headed, or what became of the deed. The river setting is vivid enough without supplying those missing details.

**Possible context note:** A “Kentucky boat” was a name for a flatboat used on inland rivers. Joseph was on the Monongahela just upstream of Pittsburgh, where that river joins the Allegheny to form the Ohio. Such boats commonly carried people and goods downstream; an early travel account describes Kentucky boats built on the Monongahela for western trade. The name does not tell us that Joseph's particular boat was bound for Kentucky, or even whether it was moving when he died. [National Park Service: flatboats](https://home.nps.gov/articles/000/flatboats-transportation-during-the-cherokee-removal-1837-1839.htm); [National Park Service: Ohio River](https://www.nps.gov/articles/the-ohio-river.htm); [François André Michaux's account, reproduced by Discover Lewis & Clark](https://lewis-clark.org/day-by-day/28-jul-1803/).

### The land and the family

Joseph's words were preserved, but the land's later history is harder to explain. Kentucky law required a land devise to be written and signed; his spoken declaration alone did not satisfy that requirement. The record does not establish whether the deed he requested had been signed, delivered, recovered, or made legally effective.

Fourteen years later, deeds concerning Highland Creek named Benjamin Meason as one of Joseph's heirs and identified children of Joseph's brothers among other claimants. Those deeds show a family inheritance network spread across several branches. They do not settle every claimant's share or identify which branch connects Benjamin to Joseph. Benjamin's parents remain unknown.

### What I take from it

The scene on the boat feels like the moment a family history changed. The records support a narrower conclusion: Joseph asked for a document that was out of reach, witnesses preserved his spoken wishes, and later relatives conveyed claimed interests in the land. What happened to the deed, and why the later title took the course it did, remain questions.

This is where my search meets the wider Meason family. Highland Creek binds Benjamin to Joseph's inheritance story without giving me the parent-child link I am looking for. The next step is to follow the deeds, the named branches, and the evidence that still might distinguish them.

## Record-reading panel for Joseph

- **What the record says:** Three witness depositions describe Joseph's final declaration aboard a Kentucky boat near Pittsburgh. The court ordered them recorded on 6 August 1799. Hartle said Joseph asked for a deed to Worthington that could not be reached.
- **What I infer:** The later Highland Creek deeds place Benjamin within Joseph's inheritance network. The deed Joseph requested could matter to the land's legal history, but its status is unknown.
- **What remains unknown:** Joseph's illness, the boat's origin and destination, the deed's execution and fate, the full title history, and Benjamin's exact branch.
